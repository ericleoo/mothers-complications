## Executive Overview

In mid-July 2026, while attending a family funeral, a 55-year-old woman (Ratna Khouw) with a history of treated hypertension experienced a hypertensive crisis and an episode of progressive left-arm numbness — a transient ischemic attack (TIA)-type presentation — that resolved only after emergency intervention. Home readings showed blood pressure at 175/95 with a heart rate of 60 despite her standing regimen of atenolol 50 mg in the morning and irbesartan 300 mg at night. The numbness spread from the left upper arm to the palm (which turned cold while the right hand stayed warm), then migrated up the back of the left arm and into the neck while in the emergency department. Sublingual captopril brought the episode under control and the numbness resolved.

The follow-up — a trip to Kuching, Sarawak on July 15 and a full diagnostic assessment on July 16, 2026, followed by a medication overhaul and titration through late July — produced a mixed but actionable picture. The brain MRI with MRA found evidence of an **old lacunar infarct** in the left basal ganglia/internal capsule plus **moderate bilateral deep white-matter ischemic changes** consistent with cerebral small-vessel disease, with no large-vessel stenosis. The abdominal ultrasound found **gallstones** (largest 2.3 × 1.4 cm) with mildly elevated cholestatic liver enzymes (GGT 89, ALP 127, ALT 35) and normal bilirubin. Reassuringly, the exercise stress test was **normal at 11.1 METs** with no ischemic ECG changes, the chest radiograph was unremarkable, the lipid profile was optimal, thyroid function was normal, tumor markers were normal, and she has documented immunity to hepatitis B.

The Kuching physicians replaced the prior antihypertensive regimen with a single-combination tablet (amlodipine 10 mg + telmisartan 80 mg) plus an antiplatelet agent (clopidogrel 75 mg) and a vasodilator/antiplatelet (cilostazol 100 mg twice daily), with rosuvastatin 20 mg at night. The amlodipine component caused a dramatic reflex tachycardia — heart rate spiked to 185, and even minor exertion pushed it to 110 — followed by three nights of stomach pain and vomiting. A local physician, Dr. Budi Gunawan, discontinued the amlodipine, kept telmisartan 80 mg, added nebivolol (Nevodio) 5 mg for heart-rate control, and started ursodeoxycholic acid (Urdafalk) for one month to dissolve the gallstones, with a repeat ultrasound planned at the one-month mark.

The new regimen then overshot in the opposite direction — half a tablet of nebivolol plus full telmisartan produced 100/58 with dizziness — before the family settled on half a tablet of nebivolol morning and night without telmisartan, achieving 115/70 with a heart rate of 72. The most recent readings (July 31, 2026) show residual variability driven by activity and anxiety — 140/80 after housework, 137/78 after deep breathing, then 160/90 under stress — managed with telmisartan 80 mg and half a tablet of bromazepam (Akamon 3 mg) as needed. The final reading after a two-hour nap is not recorded in the source. Mama later confirmed (August 3, 2026, Part 7) that the telmisartan is exactly what makes her dizzy — her blood pressure drops too low — first-person confirmation of the rescue-telmisartan problem (read precisely: telmisartan 80 mg — the maximum dose — taken erratically; a dose/pattern finding, not a class verdict — see Part 7). Mama later confirmed (August 3, 2026, Part 8), in answer to the same-day question raised in Part 7, that she **does** take the racikan and the Akamon on the same day — last Friday she took half an Akamon after panicking at a 140 reading and the racikan again that night before bed — so continuous benzodiazepine coverage on dual antiplatelets is now a documented fact, not a hypothesis.

**Amlodipine (Amlotel) — attributed by Mama to the racing heart; nebivolol now slowing it — but patient attribution is not rhythm documentation.** Mama attributes the fast heartbeat to Amlotel ("obat amlotel yang dari kucing itu" — her own way of identifying the box), confirms she no longer takes it, and states that after the switch to Nevodio (nebivolol) the heart rate is slower ("jadi detak lebih pelan"). Useful — but this is a *temporal association reported by the patient*, which is the same attribution Dr. Budi Gunawan already made in July. **No new information about rhythm has been added**, and a patient cannot distinguish sinus tachycardia from SVT or AF. Two things in the existing record argue against the amlodipine explanation: (1) **the magnitudes don't match** — HR 185 at rest alongside "even minor exertion pushed it to 110" is not what dihydropyridine reflex tachycardia looks like (that typically adds 10–25 bpm, i.e. the 110 figure); 185 sits in the range of AVNRT/AVRT, atrial flutter, or AF with rapid ventricular response; (2) **the free cross-check is already in this file** — the July 16 treadmill test (§2.5) recorded a **peak heart rate of 148 bpm** at 11.1 METs; her age-predicted maximum is ~165 (220 − 55), so a *resting* 185 sits above her observed peak exercise HR and above her physiological sinus ceiling — **sinus tachycardia at 185 is effectively excluded, making the episode an undocumented arrhythmia on existing data, at zero cost**. The unifying hypothesis connecting this to the laterality puzzle in Finding 1: **paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** — it explains both the left-arm event with a *left*-sided old infarct (cardioembolic TIA) and a resting HR of 185 (rapid ventricular response), and if correct it changes management from clopidogrel + cilostazol to anticoagulation. **The 14-day ambulatory rhythm patch is therefore a Section 0 priority at the next cardiology visit, not a completeness check.**

**The compounded anxiety medication ("obat cemas racik") — contents now documented.** The racikan contains exactly two ingredients:

- **CLOFRITIS (clobazam) 10 mg tablet — 5 tablets**
- **SERTRALINE 50 mg tablet (FAHRENHEIT brand) — 5 tablets**

Mama reports that this medication makes her dizzy/groggy ("puyeng") in the morning when she takes it, and the feeling is gone by afternoon ("sore sudah tidak puyeng"). Pharmacologically this pattern fits **clobazam**: a *long-acting* benzodiazepine (elimination half-life ~36–42 hours; active metabolite N-desmethylclobazam ~71–82 hours) that accumulates and produces a morning "hangover" that clears as the day goes on. The 5-tablet quantity implies a short, time-limited course — **revised in Part 8: the course was still ongoing on the July 31 Friday ("minum lg"), so treat the quantity as uninformative until the prescriber confirms course length and stop date; if the racikan has been nightly across multiple dispensings, the sertraline is no longer subtherapeutic but a real SSRI course (see PROGRAM_OPUS Section 6)**. The old "5 tablets is not a therapeutic SSRI course" conclusion therefore holds only if the course was short — and the evidence now points the other way. If anxiety is chronic, the proper approach is to **unbundle the racikan — continue sertraline 50 mg as a standalone daily tablet, taper the clobazam out from underneath it** (with a doctor), plus CBT, per PROGRAM_OPUS Section 6.

**Akamon 3 (bromazepam 3 mg) — PRN pattern confirmed, no dizziness — but read the two benzodiazepines together.** Mama says she is instructed to take **half a tablet only when needed**, and that it does not make her dizzy. This is consistent with the July 31 narrative (half a tablet used after the 160/90 reading). **Assessed separately, each benzo is defensible. Assessed together, they are the headline:** the racikan's **clobazam 10 mg** is *long-acting* (t½ 36–42 h; active metabolite N-desmethylclobazam 71–82 h) and accumulates over ~a week — the morning "puyeng" — while the **bromazepam ½ PRN** is taken at moments of anxiety, i.e. exactly when a BP reading alarms her. **Nothing in the record establishes she never takes both on the same day** — *now answered: she does (Part 8, August 3, 2026)*. If the racikan is a morning dose and the bromazepam an afternoon anxiety response, she has continuous benzodiazepine coverage — in a 55-year-old with white-matter disease **on dual antiplatelet therapy**, the classic setup for a fall with head impact becoming a hemorrhage (PROGRAM_OPUS 0.7). **Ask her directly this week: do you ever take the racikan and the Akamon on the same day?** *(Answered — see Part 8: last Friday she took bromazepam ½ after panicking at a 140 reading and the racikan again at night. **The morning "puyeng" — benign as to cause, not benign as to consequence.** The clobazam hangover explains the symptom without invoking hypotension (an orthostatic check on a puyeng morning would confirm that), but a groggy patient walking to the bathroom on tile is a fall-risk window regardless of why she's groggy — and §4.3 calls fall prevention non-negotiable. On a puyeng morning: sit on the edge of the bed for a full minute before standing, no stairs unaccompanied, no driving.

**The racikan's format is the deeper problem.** The correct regimen for anxiety is an SSRI daily long-term, with a benzodiazepine as a **2–4 week bridge that is then tapered off *while the SSRI continues***. A compounded capsule fusing clobazam + sertraline makes that impossible — she cannot stop the benzo without stopping the antidepressant, or continue the antidepressant without continuing the benzo. Whatever the prescriber intended, the delivery format forecloses the only sensible trajectory; raise this specifically with the prescriber. **Also establish who prescribed the racikan** — if it wasn't Dr. Budi Gunawan, no single physician currently holds her whole list, which is the actual point of reconciliation (PROGRAM_OPUS 0.2).

**Exercise preferences — plank and wall sit; wall sit is a first-line BP lever, not just a tolerated hobby.** Mama volunteers that she likes both. Isometric resistance training has the **largest resting-BP reduction of any exercise modality** in recent network meta-analyses — on the order of **8/4 mmHg, ahead of aerobic training** — and **wall squats were the top-performing individual exercise** in that literature. She has volunteered, unprompted, that she likes the single best-evidenced non-pharmacological BP intervention available to her; this belongs in the §1.4 lever table with an effect size, next to salt reduction (see PROGRAM_OPUS §1.4). Standard protocol: **4 × 2-minute holds, ~1–2 min rest between, 3×/week**, breathing continuously throughout (no breath-holding/Valsalva), stop if dizzy, no BP measurement within 30 minutes. Keep **plank secondary** — more Valsalva-prone and less well studied for BP.
